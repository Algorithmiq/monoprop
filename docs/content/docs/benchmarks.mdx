`monoprop` carries two kinds of benchmark: an **external** comparison against other open-source
Pauli and Majorana propagation engines, and an **internal** pytest suite that guards against
regressions on every commit.

## Results

![Time per step for monoprop against other Pauli and Majorana propagation engines](/benchmarks/headline_runtime.png)

Wall-clock time per step on a log axis, so the vertical gap between two curves is the speed-up.
On the Pauli side `monoprop` is **267-325× faster than the other CPU engines** and 7.8× faster
than the GPU engine; on the Majorana side it is **27.7× faster** than `MajoranaPropagation.jl`.
It is also the leanest in memory in both. Every engine runs the identical model, all on
[Leonardo (CINECA)](https://docs.hpc.cineca.it/hpc/leonardo.html); the sections below give the
numbers and [Methods](#methods) the setup.

### Pauli propagation

Trotterized time evolution of a 2D transverse-field Ising model, measuring the `ZZ` correlator on
a fixed qubit pair. Two sweeps: one grows the circuit depth at a fixed lattice, the other grows
the lattice at a fixed depth.

#### Fixed lattice, increasing depth

A 12×12 lattice (144 qubits) over 28 Trotter points, `dt = 0.05`, coefficients truncated below
`1e-6`, no weight cutoff. The operator reaches ~33M terms.

| engine | final step | vs `monoprop` | peak RSS | vs `monoprop` |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| `monoprop` | 1.56 s | — | 4275 MB | — |
| `cuPauliProp (GPU)` | 12.23 s | 7.8× | 11652 MB *(device)* | 2.7× |
| `QuEra ppvm` | 417.29 s | 267× | 6956 MB | 1.6× |
| `PauliPropagation.jl` | 433.16 s | 277× | 11102 MB | 2.6× |
| `Qiskit pauli-prop` | 507.74 s | 325× | 34270 MB | 8.0× |

All engines agree on the observable to within 3.8e-4 except `Qiskit pauli-prop`, which truncates
on a term budget rather than a coefficient threshold and lands 2.8e-3 away. `cuPauliProp`'s host
RSS sits flat at 552 MB because its operator is on the card, so its device peak is quoted instead.

![Runtime and peak RSS per Trotter step for five Pauli propagation engines](/benchmarks/pauli_results.png)

Solid lines are peak RSS; the faint companions are each engine's own accounting, for context only.

#### Increasing lattice size

The complement: circuit depth and truncation held fixed while the lattice grows from 6×6 (36
qubits) to 18×18 (324 qubits). Each engine runs the identical model at each size in its own
subprocess, with a **300 s budget per point**.

![Total runtime versus lattice size, monoprop, PauliPropagation.jl and cuPauliProp](/benchmarks/pauli_scaling_runtime.png)

At a fixed coefficient truncation the operator **saturates**: past ~100 qubits every engine
carries the same ~55.0M terms whatever the lattice. This therefore measures *cost per term kept*,
not a growing problem, which is why `monoprop`'s total runtime rises only from 6.35 s to 14.1 s
(2.2×) across a 9× larger lattice.

At this depth the 300 s budget is binding for the single-core engines: `QuEra ppvm` and `Qiskit
pauli-prop` do not finish even 6×6, and `PauliPropagation.jl` stops after 10×10. Their curves end
where they ran out of budget, not where the engines stop working.

![Peak working memory versus lattice size, monoprop, PauliPropagation.jl and cuPauliProp](/benchmarks/pauli_scaling_memory_working_set.png)

Each engine is read against the pool its operator actually lives in. For the CPU engines that
**is** the peak host RSS above; only `cuPauliProp` differs, contributing its device peak instead.
`monoprop` goes from 5045 MB to 7835 MB (1.55×) across the full ladder, while `cuPauliProp`
climbs from 7.6 GB to **34.4 GB of the A100's 64 GB**, 4.5× `monoprop`'s host footprint at 324
qubits and within sight of the card's limit.

![Bar chart of monoprop's speed-up over each engine, grouped by qubit count](/benchmarks/pauli_speedup.png)

The speed-up widens with size in both directions: 14× to 31× over `PauliPropagation.jl` across
the sizes it finishes, and 4.2× to 20× over the GPU engine across the full ladder.

### Majorana propagation

Trotterized time evolution of the 1D Fermi-Hubbard model at 60 spinful sites over 20 layers,
tracking runtime, expectation value, term count and memory at each layer. Both engines at 24
threads.

![Term count, expectation value, runtime and peak RSS versus layer, monoprop vs MajoranaPropagation.jl](/benchmarks/majorana_results.png)

Both engines simulate the same physics: the expectation values agree to within 2.0e-6 at every
layer. `monoprop` keeps 1.38× more terms at equal truncation (109.4M against 79.4M), and still
finishes the 20 layers in **57.1 s against 1583.7 s**, a 27.7× speed-up, and 27.9× on the final
layer alone (22.9 s against 639.4 s). Peak RSS is **8.8 GB against 17.5 GB**, half the footprint
for a third more terms retained.

![Runtime versus circuit depth on linear axes, monoprop vs MajoranaPropagation.jl](/benchmarks/majorana_runtime.png)

Runtime also gets its own linear-axis figure, since it is the quantity most often cited alone.
`monoprop`'s curve lies along the bottom of that axis, annotated with the top of the band so that
flat reads as small rather than as zero.

### Caveats

- **Only two of the four CPU engines use more than one core.** `QuEra ppvm` and Qiskit's
  `pauli-prop` propagate on a single core, measured at 0.99 busy cores mid-propagation, and no
  environment variable changes it: ppvm's rayon-parallel `PauliSum` lives in its `config::dashmap`
  variant while its Python bindings expose only `indexmap`, and Qiskit's `_accelerate` extension
  has no parallel path at all. Their ratios are wall-clock, not per-core, which is most of why
  they are the two that exhaust the budget first.
- **The Julia engines run in their fastest documented configuration**, the vector-backed
  containers (`VectorPauliSum` under `Performance.propagate!`, needing the dev branch 0.8.0+, and
  `VectorMajoranaSum` for AcceleratedKernels). The dict-backed containers are serial regardless of
  thread count. The vector path leaves duplicate strings unmerged, so its term count is a storage
  count rather than an operator size.
- **The comparison against `cuPauliProp` is across machines**, as any CPU-vs-GPU comparison must
  be. Every engine's own points come from a single node, which is the part that has to hold for a
  scaling curve to mean anything, and the plotting scripts refuse to draw a curve mixing hosts.

## Methods

Standalone scripts driving every engine live in
[`packages/bench-third-party`](https://github.com/Algorithmiq/monoprop/tree/main/packages/bench-third-party),
a standalone `uv` project with its own lockfile, deliberately outside the repository workspace: it
pins CUDA-specific wheels and a narrower Python range than `monoprop` itself.

### Hardware

Measured on [Leonardo (CINECA)](https://docs.hpc.cineca.it/hpc/leonardo.html), on exclusive nodes.
The GPU engine cannot share the CPU node, since it needs a card and Leonardo's accelerated
partition is a different node shape, so it ran separately:

| | node | hardware | engines |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CPU | `lrdn4635` (DCGP) | 2× Intel Xeon Platinum 8480+, 112 cores, ~480 GB RAM | `monoprop` (56 threads), `PauliPropagation.jl` (28), `QuEra ppvm`, `Qiskit pauli-prop` |
| GPU | `lrdn0219` (Booster) | NVIDIA A100-SXM-64GB, 8 host cores | `cuPauliProp` |

Read it off the data rather than this table: each record carries its own `host`, `threads` and
`library_version`, and the scripts print that provenance beside the numbers. Measured with
`monoprop 0.8.1.dev61+gcb9a033aa`, Julia 1.12.6, `PauliPropagation.jl` 0.8.2.

### How memory is measured

Memory on this page means **peak resident set size**, read from the kernel's `VmHWM` high-water
mark. It is the number to quote, and the only one that is like-for-like against a library written
in another language. The one exception is the GPU engine, whose operator is not in host memory at
all.

- **It is exact, not sampled.** The kernel updates `VmHWM` on every RSS increase, so no transient
  can be missed and no background thread is needed, which also means the GIL cannot hide anything
  from it.
- **The window is reset per step.** Each measured region settles the process first (`gc.collect()`
  + `malloc_trim`, or `GC.gc()` + `malloc_trim` on the Julia side) and then resets `VmHWM` to that
  floor, so the peak reported is that step's own and not an earlier step's retained garbage.
- **Every engine is measured by the same instrument**, from outside, over exactly the timed region.

It is a whole-process figure, so it includes the interpreter and the loaded libraries. That
constant is why the curves are worth reading for their *growth*, and why an engine can be leaner
in RSS while being far slower.

Where an engine also accounts for itself (`monoprop`'s `operator_memory_bytes()`,
`PauliPropagation.jl`'s `Base.summarysize`, cuPauliProp's device pool) that figure is recorded
alongside as `native_memory` and drawn as a faint companion line. **Those are not comparable with
each other**: they count different things in different pools. They are context for a single
engine's curve, never a cross-engine ranking.

### Reproducing

Every engine is driven from one `uv` project:

```bash
cd packages/bench-third-party
uv sync    # monoprop (editable), qiskit, ppvm, pauli-prop, cuquantum-python-cu12
```

The Julia engines are optional — the Python comparisons run without them:

```bash
curl -fsSL https://install.julialang.org | sh          # juliaup + latest stable Julia
julia -e 'using Pkg; Pkg.add(["JSON", "ProgressMeter"])'
```

Then, per benchmark:

```bash
# Pauli, fixed lattice — edit settings.json first to change the model
cd pauli_prop
uv run python run_model.py        # CPU engines -> results.json
julia run_model.jl                # optional; merges PauliPropagation.jl in
uv run python run_model.py --backends cupauliprop -o results_gpu.json   # on a GPU node
uv run python plot_results.py

# Pauli, lattice ladder — 300 s budget per (size, engine)
uv run python run_scaling.py --output scaling_cpu.jsonl --timeout 300 \
    --backends monoprop juliapp ppvm qiskit --threads monoprop=56 juliapp=28
uv run python run_scaling.py --output scaling_gpu.jsonl --timeout 300 \
    --backends cupauliprop                                   # on a GPU node

# CPU file first: where a backend appears in both, the first file wins
uv run python plot_scaling.py scaling_cpu.jsonl scaling_gpu.jsonl
uv run python plot_scaling.py scaling_cpu.jsonl scaling_gpu.jsonl \
    --memory-key working_set_MB
uv run python plot_speedup.py scaling_cpu.jsonl scaling_gpu.jsonl

# Majorana
cd ../majorana_prop
monoprop_NUM_THREADS=24 JULIA_NUM_THREADS=24 bash run_benchmarks.sh

# The two-panel headline figure, from both benchmarks' committed results
cd .. && uv run python plot_headline.py
```

The GPU sweep runs on its own node, so it lands in its own file and is combined at plot time.
Order matters on that command line: where a backend appears in both files the **first** file
wins, which is what keeps `monoprop` on the CPU node rather than silently taking some of its
points from the GPU node's host. `plot_scaling.py` warns if any single backend's curve ends up
spanning two hosts, and refuses to label a memory axis "host RSS" if the records say the figure
was measured some other way.

All Pauli model parameters live in
[`settings.json`](https://github.com/Algorithmiq/monoprop/blob/main/packages/bench-third-party/pauli_prop/settings.json)
and are shared by every engine, so one edit compares them all on an identical problem instance.
The output schemas, the full field lists and the per-engine caveats are documented in
[`packages/bench-third-party/README.md`](https://github.com/Algorithmiq/monoprop/tree/main/packages/bench-third-party).

## Internal benchmarks

A pytest suite measuring the **time** and **peak RSS** of `monoprop`'s core operations. It is
kept apart from the test suite and run with `just bench`.

### Running

Each run takes a **label** and becomes one column in `results/REPORT.md`, so serial, MPI and
threaded variants sit side by side. Extra arguments are forwarded to pytest.

```bash
uv sync --group bench                          # once

just bench serial                              # serial run, column "serial"
just bench-smoke                               # quick sanity check (tiny sizes)
just bench serial --num-modes 64 --bench-rounds 10

monoprop_NUM_THREADS=10 just bench serial-t10  # cap the partition count

uv run --group bench monoprop-bench-report benches/results  # rebuild report, no re-run
```

Two suites are available:

- **`bench_random.py`** — `build_graph`, `pare`, `energy`, `gradient` and `inplace` on a random
  problem. Configurable with `--gen-length` (4), `--obs-terms` (10000), `--num-generators`
  (100), `--num-modes` (128), `--cutoff` (6), `--seed` (0) and `--bench-rounds` (1); defaults in
  parentheses.
- **`bench_models.py`** — fixed models, marked `slow`: a 120-qubit Fermi-Hubbard 29-step Trotter
  run (`test_model[hubbard]`) and a 127-qubit kicked-Ising run over 20 layers
  (`test_model[pauli]`). Override any config field with `--<model>-<field>`, e.g.
  `--pauli-num-layers 30`; `just bench --help` lists them all.

Each run writes `results/time-<label>.json` (pytest-benchmark) and `<label>.json` (everything
else); `monoprop-bench-report` merges every label into `results/REPORT.md`.

### MPI

The suite is communicator-aware: operations are barrier-wrapped so the timed cost is the
**makespan** across ranks, and memory is the **peak of the summed live PSS**, replayed from
per-rank timelines. Summing each rank's independently-timed peak would count transients that
never coexisted; PSS rather than RSS so that a shared page is counted once rather than once per
rank.

```bash
just bench-build-mpi                           # build once (MPI on)
monoprop_NUM_THREADS=2 just bench-mpi r5t2 5 --map-by slot:PE=2 --bind-to core
```

### Continuous benchmarking

Every commit on `main` runs `bench_main.yml`, and the results are tracked by
[Bencher](https://bencher.dev/), which graphs them over time and alerts when a metric drifts
outside its threshold. The workflow runs the same recipes you can run locally:

```bash
just bench-ci ci-linux          # default sizes, more rounds, no slow models
just bench-bmf ci-linux         # the same results as Bencher Metric Format JSON
```

`monoprop-bench-bmf` merges both artifacts into one report because Bencher accepts a single
adapter per upload — the stock `python_pytest` adapter would keep the timings and drop
everything else. Four measures are tracked:

| Measure | Unit | Source | Threshold |
|---|---|---|---|
| `latency` | nanoseconds | pytest-benchmark mean ± 1σ | Student's t-test, upper 0.99 |
| `peak-memory` | bytes | per-operation peak RSS (`VmHWM`) | +10% |
| `resting-memory` | bytes | settled operator + graph footprint | +10% |
| `terms` | count | terms in the evolved operator | any change |

`terms` is deterministic for a fixed seed and problem size, so it is held to an exact match: any
drift is an algorithmic change and should be a deliberate one. Timings are noisy on shared
runners, so alerts on `main` are recorded but do not fail the build.

#### Testbeds

Bencher keys every metric on (branch, testbed, measure), so a testbed is one machine shape and
its history is only meaningful against itself. Changing the hardware under a testbed name shows
up as a step change in every series, which is why each machine gets its own name.

| Workflow | Runner | Testbed | Label | Profile |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `bench_main.yml` | GitHub-hosted `ubuntu-26.04` | `ubuntu-26.04` | `ci-linux` | `not slow`, 5 rounds |
| `bench_bare_metal.yml` | [RunsOn](https://runs-on.com/) EC2 node | `aws-c7i-4xlarge` | `ci-bare-metal` | everything, 3 rounds |

The bare-metal workflow provisions a dedicated node per run, so nothing else is scheduled on it
while the benchmarks run. That lower noise floor is the point: it is what a testbed needs before
its timing thresholds can be tightened enough to gate on, and it is why it can afford the slow
fixed models the shared runner skips.

Which benchmarks run, and how many rounds each gets, are the `bench-markers` and `bench-rounds`
inputs of the reusable `bench.yml`. Changing either for a testbed that already has history
rewrites what its series measures, so pick them before the first run:

```bash
monoprop_BENCH_MARKERS= monoprop_BENCH_ROUNDS=3 just bench-ci ci-bare-metal
```
